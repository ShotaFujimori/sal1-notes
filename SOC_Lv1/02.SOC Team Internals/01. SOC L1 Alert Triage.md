# 54.SAL1 — SOC L1 Alert Triage — Cheat Sheet

**Focus:** Understanding SOC alerts, their properties, prioritization methodology, and systematic triage procedures for L1 analysts.

---

## TL;DR

* **Alert Function:** Alerts save SOC analysts from manually reviewing millions of logs by highlighting only suspicious or anomalous events, reducing daily workload from millions of events to dozens of actionable alerts.
* **Alert Lifecycle:** Events occur → Systems log events → Logs ship to SIEM/EDR → Detection rules generate alerts → L1 analysts triage → L2 analysts handle escalations.
* **L1 Primary Role:** SOC L1 analysts are the first line of defense, responsible for distinguishing real threats (True Positives) from noise (False Positives) and escalating genuine threats to L2 analysts.
* **Prioritization Formula:** Filter for new alerts first, then sort by severity (Critical → High → Medium → Low), then by time (oldest first).
* **Triage Workflow:** Assign alert → Move to In Progress → Investigate using workbooks or systematic analysis → Determine verdict → Comment with reasoning → Close alert.
* **Critical Success Factor:** Proper alert triage determines whether cyberattacks are detected and prevented or missed entirely, making this an essential skill for SOC operations.

---

## Core Concepts

### 1. From Events to Alerts

**Event Generation Flow:**

The alert generation process follows a systematic pipeline that transforms raw system activities into actionable security notifications.

**Step-by-Step Process:**
1. **Event Occurs:** User login, process launch, file download, network connection, or other system activity takes place
2. **System Logs Event:** Operating system, firewall, cloud provider, or application records the activity
3. **Log Shipping:** All system logs are transmitted to centralized security solutions (SIEM or EDR)
4. **Alert Generation:** Detection rules analyze logs and generate alerts for suspicious or anomalous patterns

**Scale Context:** SOC teams receive millions of logs per day from thousands of different systems. Most events are expected and benign, but some require immediate attention. Alerts filter this massive volume down to dozens of manageable notifications per day.

**Definition of Alert:** A notification generated by a security solution when a specific event or sequence of events occurs that matches predefined detection logic indicating potential security concern.

---

### 2. Alert Management Platforms

**Platform Types and Use Cases:**

Organizations use different platforms for alert management based on their size, complexity, and operational maturity.

**SIEM Systems (Primary Choice):**
- **Examples:** Splunk Enterprise Security, Elastic Security
- **Capabilities:** Solid alert management capabilities with centralized log analysis
- **Best For:** Most SOC teams of any size
- **Features:** Rule-based detection, correlation, dashboards, case management

**EDR/NDR Systems (Endpoint/Network Detection):**
- **Examples:** Microsoft Defender, CrowdStrike Falcon
- **Capabilities:** Provide their own alert dashboards focused on endpoint or network events
- **Limitation:** Preferred to aggregate alerts into SIEM or SOAR rather than managing in multiple dashboards

**SOAR Systems (Security Orchestration and Automation):**
- **Examples:** Splunk SOAR, Cortex SOAR
- **Capabilities:** Aggregate and centralize alerts from multiple security solutions
- **Best For:** Bigger SOC teams with complex infrastructure

**ITSM Systems (Ticket Management):**
- **Examples:** Jira, TheHive, Trello (adapted)
- **Capabilities:** Custom ticket management workflows
- **Best For:** Teams needing specialized case tracking beyond standard SIEM features

---

### 3. SOC Team Roles in Alert Triage

**Role Distribution and Responsibilities:**

Alert triage involves the entire SOC team, with each role contributing specific expertise to the detection and response process.

**SOC L1 Analysts (First Line of Defense):**
- Review incoming alerts from the queue
- Distinguish malicious activity (True Positives) from benign events (False Positives)
- Conduct initial investigation using workbooks and technical analysis
- Notify and escalate to L2 analysts when real threats are confirmed
- Document findings and triage reasoning in alert comments
- May receive zero to one hundred alerts per day depending on organization size

**SOC L2 Analysts (Deep Investigation):**
- Receive escalated alerts from L1 analysts
- Perform deeper technical analysis using advanced tools and techniques
- Execute remediation actions to contain and eliminate threats
- Coordinate with IT teams for system changes
- Provide guidance to L1 analysts on complex cases

**SOC Engineers (Detection Engineering):**
- Design and tune detection rules to minimize false positives
- Ensure alerts contain sufficient information for efficient triage
- Develop workbooks and playbooks for common alert types
- Maintain and optimize SIEM/EDR configurations

**SOC Manager (Quality and Performance):**
- Track speed and quality metrics of alert triage
- Ensure real attacks are not missed due to process failures
- Manage team workload and resource allocation
- Report on SOC effectiveness to leadership

## Alert Properties (Field Reference)

Understanding alert properties is essential for effective triage and communication within the SOC team.

### Property 1: Alert Time

**Description:** Shows alert creation timestamp. Note that alerts usually trigger a few minutes after the actual event occurs due to processing delays in the detection pipeline.

**Example:**
- Alert Time: March 21, 15:35 (when alert was created)
- Event Time: March 21, 15:32 (when actual suspicious activity occurred)

**Importance:** Understanding this delay helps analysts correlate alerts with logs and understand attack timelines.

---

### Property 2: Alert Name

**Description:** Provides a concise summary of the detected activity, based on the detection rule's name. This is typically the first field analysts read when scanning the alert queue.

**Common Examples:**
- "Unusual Login Location" (geographic anomaly detection)
- "Email Marked as Phishing" (email security alert)
- "Windows RDP Bruteforce" (authentication attack)
- "Potential Data Exfiltration" (data loss prevention)
- "Unapproved Mimikatz Usage" (credential dumping tool detection)
- "Double-Extension File Creation" (malware delivery technique)

**Usage:** Alert names should immediately convey the type of threat and affected system or service.

---

### Property 3: Alert Severity

**Description:** Defines the urgency and potential impact of the alert. Initially set by detection engineers based on risk assessment, but can be adjusted by analysts during triage if the initial severity classification proves incorrect.

**Severity Levels:**
- 🔴 **Critical / Urgent:** Immediate action required, likely ongoing breach or imminent major impact
- 🟠 **High / Severe:** Significant threat requiring prompt investigation within hours
- 🟡 **Medium / Moderate:** Notable security concern requiring investigation within the day
- 🟢 **Low / Informational:** Minor anomaly or informational alert for awareness

**Prioritization Impact:** Critical alerts are designed to be much more likely to represent real, major threats with significant business impact compared to medium or low severity alerts.

---

### Property 4: Alert Status

**Description:** Tracks the current state of alert handling, informing the team whether someone is working on the alert or if triage is complete.

**Common Status Values:**
- 🆕 **New / Unassigned:** Alert just generated, not yet assigned to any analyst
- 🔄 **In Progress / Pending:** Analyst actively investigating the alert
- ✅ **Closed / Resolved:** Triage completed, verdict determined, no further action needed
- **Custom Statuses:** Many organizations add additional statuses like "Escalated," "Awaiting Information," or "On Hold"

**Workflow Importance:** Status prevents multiple analysts from duplicating investigation work on the same alert.

---

### Property 5: Alert Verdict (Classification)

**Description:** Explains whether the alert represents a real security threat or is a false alarm. This is the most critical determination made during alert triage.

**Primary Verdicts:**
- 🔴 **True Positive / Real Threat:** Confirmed malicious activity requiring escalation and response
- 🟢 **False Positive / No Threat:** Benign activity that triggered detection rules incorrectly

**Additional Verdicts (Organization-Specific):**
- **Benign Positive:** Alert triggered correctly but activity is authorized (e.g., penetration testing)
- **Inconclusive:** Insufficient information to make determination
- **True Positive - No Action Required:** Threat confirmed but already automatically remediated

**Impact:** This verdict determines whether L2 analysts are engaged and incident response procedures are initiated.

---

### Property 6: Alert Assignee (Owner)

**Description:** Shows the analyst who was assigned or who assigned themselves to review the alert. The assignee takes full responsibility for proper triage and documentation.

**Alternative Names:** Alert owner, responsible analyst, handler

**Purpose:** Establishes accountability and prevents conflicting actions from multiple analysts working the same alert simultaneously.

---

### Property 7: Alert Description

**Description:** Provides detailed context about the alert, usually organized into three sections displayed on the right side of the alert interface.

**Standard Description Sections:**

**Section 1: Detection Logic**
- Explains the rule logic that generated the alert
- Details the conditions that must be met for the alert to trigger
- May include technical details about thresholds or correlation logic

**Section 2: Threat Context**
- Describes why this activity pattern can indicate an attack
- References known attack techniques or threat actor behaviors
- Explains potential business impact if the threat is real

**Section 3: Triage Guidance (Optional)**
- Provides specific investigation steps for this alert type
- Lists key indicators to examine
- Suggests tools or queries to use during investigation

---

### Property 8: Alert Fields

**Description:** Provides technical details and IOCs (Indicators of Compromise) on which the alert was triggered, along with SOC analyst comments.

**Common Field Examples:**
- **Affected Hostname:** Computer or server involved in the suspicious activity
- **Affected User:** Account name that performed or was targeted by the activity
- **Source IP Address:** Origin point of network connections
- **Destination IP Address:** Target of network connections
- **Process Name:** Executable that triggered the detection
- **Process User:** Account under which the process executed
- **Command Line:** Full command executed (critical for malware analysis)
- **Target File:** File path involved in suspicious operations
- **File Hash (MD5/SHA256):** Cryptographic fingerprint for malware identification
- **URL/Domain:** Web addresses involved in network connections
- **Email Subject/Sender:** For phishing and email-based threats

**Usage:** These fields provide the raw data needed for deep investigation and correlation with threat intelligence.

## Alert Prioritization Methodology

Deciding which alert to handle first is crucial for ensuring timely detection of real threats, especially when facing a queue of dozens or hundreds of alerts.

### Step 1: Filter for New Alerts

**Command/Action:** Apply dashboard filters to show only alerts with "New" or "Unassigned" status.

**Rationale:** Do not take alerts that other analysts have already reviewed or are currently investigating. This prevents duplicated effort and ensures efficient resource utilization.

**Dashboard Filter Example:**
```
Status: New OR Unassigned
Assignee: None OR Empty
```

---

### Step 2: Sort by Severity (Primary Sort)

**Command/Action:** Sort filtered alerts by severity level in descending order.

**Priority Order:**
1. Critical alerts (🔴)
2. High alerts (🟠)
3. Medium alerts (🟡)
4. Low alerts (🟢)

**Rationale:** Detection engineers design rules such that critical alerts are much more likely to be real, major threats and cause significantly more business impact than medium or low severity alerts. Addressing critical threats first minimizes potential damage.

---

### Step 3: Sort by Time (Secondary Sort)

**Command/Action:** Within each severity level, sort alerts by creation time in ascending order (oldest first).

**Rationale:** If both alerts represent genuine breaches, the attacker associated with the older alert has had more time to move laterally, escalate privileges, and exfiltrate data. The "newcomer" from the newer alert has likely just started reconnaissance. Addressing older alerts first minimizes attacker dwell time.

**Prioritization Formula Summary:**
```
Filter: Status = New/Unassigned
Primary Sort: Severity DESC (Critical → Low)
Secondary Sort: Time ASC (Oldest → Newest)
```

## Alert Triage Workflow (Step-by-Step Process)

This systematic approach ensures consistent, thorough investigation of every alert and proper documentation for audit and learning purposes.

### Phase 1: Initial Actions

**Purpose:** Take ownership of the alert and prepare for investigation.

**Step 1: Assign Alert to Yourself**
- Action: Click the alert and set yourself as the assignee
- Purpose: Establishes accountability and prevents other analysts from working the same alert
- Verification: Your name should appear in the "Assignee" field

**Step 2: Change Status to "In Progress"**
- Action: Update alert status from "New" to "In Progress"
- Purpose: Signals to team members that this alert is being actively investigated
- Visibility: Other analysts can see this alert is no longer available for triage

**Step 3: Familiarize with Alert Details**
- Review alert name and understand the detection type
- Read the full alert description including detection logic and threat context
- Note key fields: affected user, hostname, IP addresses, file paths, command lines
- Identify the time window of suspicious activity

---

### Phase 2: Investigation (Core Analysis)

**Purpose:** Apply technical knowledge to determine if the activity is malicious or benign.

This phase is the most complex and requires applying technical expertise. Many SOC teams develop workbooks (also called playbooks or runbooks) providing specific investigation instructions for each alert category.

**Investigation Framework (When Workbooks Unavailable):**

**Step 1: Identify the Threat Target**
- Determine who or what is under threat
- Common targets: specific user account, hostname, cloud resource, network segment, website
- Example: "User M.Clark on laptop DESKTOP-ABC123"

**Step 2: Understand the Suspicious Action**
- Clarify what activity triggered the alert
- Categories: suspicious login, malware execution, phishing attempt, data exfiltration, privilege escalation
- Example: "Unusual login from geographic location outside normal user pattern"

**Step 3: Review Surrounding Events**
- Query SIEM/EDR for events shortly before and after the alert
- Look for: process execution chains, network connections, file modifications, authentication attempts
- Time window: typically examine 15-30 minutes before and after

**Common SIEM Query Patterns:**

```
# Example Splunk query for surrounding events
index=security user="M.Clark" 
earliest=-30m@s latest=+30m@s
| table _time, action, src_ip, dest_ip, process_name

# Example Elastic query for process execution
event.category: process AND user.name: "M.Clark" 
AND @timestamp: ["now-30m" TO "now+30m"]

# Look for lateral movement after suspicious login
index=security dest_ip="10.0.0.50" action=login
| stats count by src_ip, user
| where count > 5
```

**Step 4: Apply Threat Intelligence**
- Use threat intelligence platforms to verify suspicious indicators
- Check IP addresses, domains, file hashes against known malicious databases
- Resources: VirusTotal, AbuseIPDB, MISP, AlienVault OTX

**Threat Intelligence Lookup Example:**
```bash
# Check file hash reputation
curl "https://www.virustotal.com/api/v3/files/{file_hash}" \
  -H "x-apikey: YOUR_API_KEY"

# Check IP reputation
whois <suspicious_ip>
curl "https://api.abuseipdb.com/api/v2/check?ipAddress=<ip>" \
  -H "Key: YOUR_API_KEY"
```

---

### Phase 3: Final Actions

**Purpose:** Document findings and close the alert with proper verdict.

**Step 1: Determine Alert Verdict**
- Decision point: Is this a True Positive (real threat) or False Positive (benign activity)?
- Consider all evidence gathered during investigation
- When uncertain, err on the side of caution and escalate to L2

**Verdict Decision Criteria:**

**True Positive Indicators:**
- Activity matches known attack patterns or TTPs
- Threat intelligence confirms malicious indicators
- User/system behavior significantly deviates from baseline
- Multiple correlated suspicious events
- Cannot find legitimate business justification

**False Positive Indicators:**
- Activity explained by legitimate business operations
- User confirms authorized action
- Known safe applications or processes
- Testing or maintenance activities
- Geographic location matches user travel or VPN usage

**Step 2: Prepare Detailed Comment**
- Summarize investigation steps taken
- List key evidence examined
- Explain verdict reasoning with specific findings
- Include any queries run or tools used
- Recommend escalation path if True Positive

**Comment Template Example:**
```
Investigation Summary:
- Alert: Unusual VPN Login Location for user M.Clark
- Source IP: 203.0.113.45 (Amsterdam, Netherlands)

Analysis Performed:
1. Verified user's normal login patterns (usually US-based)
2. Contacted user directly - confirmed traveling for business
3. Checked surrounding events - no suspicious activity post-login
4. IP reputation clean on AbuseIPDB and VirusTotal

Verdict: False Positive
Reasoning: User confirmed authorized travel. No suspicious 
activity detected in 1-hour window post-login. Standard 
email access only, no administrative actions.

Recommendation: No escalation required. Consider updating 
user profile with expected travel locations.
```

**Step 3: Update Alert Status to Closed**
- Return to the alert dashboard
- Change status from "In Progress" to "Closed" or "Resolved"
- Ensure verdict and comment are saved
- Verify timestamp is recorded for metrics

**Step 4: Verify Completion**
- Check if system provides a completion flag or confirmation
- If flag received, alert triage was successful
- If no flag, review values set and potentially reset dashboard to retry

## Gotchas & Pitfalls

**Alert vs. Event Time Confusion:** Analysts sometimes correlate alerts with logs using the alert creation time rather than the actual event time. Remember that alerts typically trigger several minutes after the underlying event occurs due to processing delays in the detection pipeline. Always use the event timestamp when correlating with logs, not the alert timestamp.

**Status Management Failures:** Forgetting to change alert status to "In Progress" when beginning investigation can result in duplicate work when another analyst picks up the same alert. Similarly, leaving alerts in "In Progress" status after completing triage creates false metrics and prevents proper queue management. Always update status at the beginning and end of your investigation.

**Insufficient Investigation Documentation:** Many junior analysts close alerts with minimal comments or generic verdicts like "False Positive - looked fine." This creates problems for metric tracking, audit trails, and learning from past incidents. Every alert should have a detailed comment explaining what was investigated, what evidence was examined, and why the verdict was chosen. Future analysts (including yourself) will need to understand your reasoning when similar alerts appear.

**Prioritization Discipline Breakdown:** When faced with multiple critical alerts, analysts sometimes grab the most interesting-sounding alert rather than the oldest one. This is particularly tempting when one alert mentions "Mimikatz" or "Ransomware" while another seems mundane like "Unusual Login." However, the mundane alert might represent the initial access that led to the more dramatic alert. Always follow the prioritization methodology: severity first, then time.

**False Sense of Security from Closed Alerts:** Closing an alert as True Positive does not stop the attack or prevent damage. Many junior analysts feel a sense of completion after clicking "Close" on a True Positive alert, but this is only the beginning of the incident response process. True Positive verdicts must trigger escalation to L2 analysts who can execute containment and remediation actions. Never close a True Positive without confirming it has been properly escalated.

**Verdict Uncertainty Paralysis:** Analysts sometimes spend excessive time trying to reach 100% certainty on a verdict, especially for complex alerts with ambiguous indicators. Remember that as an L1 analyst, your role is initial triage, not complete investigation. If you cannot determine the verdict with reasonable confidence after appropriate investigation time, escalate to L2 for deeper analysis. It is better to over-escalate than to miss a real threat.

**Workbook Dependency:** Teams with detailed workbooks sometimes produce analysts who cannot triage alerts effectively when workbooks are unavailable or when novel alert types appear. While workbooks are valuable tools, analysts must also develop independent investigation skills and critical thinking. Practice the systematic investigation framework even for routine alerts to build these muscles.

**Surrounding Event Neglect:** Many analysts focus exclusively on the specific event that triggered the alert without examining what happened before or after. However, context is critical. A suspicious login might look benign in isolation but becomes clearly malicious when you see the privilege escalation attempt five minutes later, or see the reconnaissance activity ten minutes prior. Always examine surrounding events in a reasonable time window.

**Threat Intelligence Misinterpretation:** Not all threat intelligence matches are equally significant. A file hash matching VirusTotal doesn't always mean compromise if only 1 out of 70 engines flagged it. Similarly, an IP address appearing in a threat feed might be a dynamic IP that was malicious in the past but is now assigned to a legitimate user. Always consider confidence levels and context when applying threat intelligence to verdict decisions.

**Communication Gaps:** L1 analysts sometimes close alerts without informing affected users or IT teams about their findings, even for False Positives. Building relationships through communication helps. When you determine a user triggered a False Positive, consider sending them a brief message explaining what happened and why. This builds trust and increases their likelihood of reporting future suspicious activity directly to the SOC.

## Key Statistics and Workflow Metrics

- SOC teams can receive **millions of logs per day** from thousands of systems
- Alerts reduce this to **dozens of alerts per day** for analyst review
- L1 analysts may receive **zero to 100 alerts per day** depending on organization size
- Alert triage is also called: alert handling, alert processing, alert investigation, or alert analysis
- Alerts typically trigger **a few minutes after** the actual event occurs
- **Every alert** can potentially reveal a cyberattack in progress

---

## Essential Workflow Principles

**Alert Ownership Accountability:** When you assign yourself to an alert, you take full responsibility for proper investigation, documentation, and escalation if needed. Never abandon an alert in progress without properly handing it off to another analyst or your shift replacement.

**Documentation as Learning Tool:** Your detailed comments serve multiple purposes beyond the immediate case. They create institutional knowledge, help tune detection rules, train new analysts, and support post-incident reviews. Write comments as if you are teaching a new analyst about this alert type.

**Continuous Improvement Mindset:** Each alert you triage provides an opportunity to improve detection logic, update workbooks, or identify gaps in visibility. When you encounter repeated False Positives on the same alert type, document patterns and work with SOC engineers to tune the rule. When you find a True Positive that nearly slipped through, document what made it difficult to detect and suggest improvements.
